## 1. Introduction

**NIST Special Publication 800-115**, *Technical Guide to Information Security Testing and Assessment*, is the standard most often required in US government, defense, and heavily regulated environments (finance, healthcare) — where a compliance framework like NIST CSF or an auditor explicitly references it. It's less a step-by-step playbook than PTES and more a formal taxonomy of testing techniques and how to plan/execute/report on them responsibly.

## 2. Three Categories of Assessment Techniques

NIST SP 800-115 groups all security testing into three technique families:

| Category | Description | Examples |
|---|---|---|
| Review Techniques | Passive, examining documentation/configuration without active testing | Log review, policy review, network sniffing, file integrity checking |
| Target Identification and Analysis Techniques | Active discovery and scanning, but stopping short of exploitation | Network discovery, port/service scanning, vulnerability scanning, wireless scanning |
| Target Vulnerability Validation Techniques | Actually confirming exploitability | Password cracking, penetration testing, social engineering |

This three-tier split is useful for **scoping conversations** — a client asking for a "vulnerability assessment" wants Category 2; a client asking for a full "penetration test" wants Category 3 (see [[Security_Testing_Types]] for the assessment-vs-pentest distinction itself).

## 3. The Four-Phase Process

```
1. Planning     — rules of engagement, scope, logistics
2. Discovery    — information gathering + scanning
3. Attack       — exploitation, gaining access, escalating
4. Reporting    — findings, risk analysis, mitigation recommendations
                  (often runs concurrently with the other three, not just at the end)
```

Note Reporting is explicitly called out as continuous, not a final afterthought — findings should be documented as they're discovered throughout Discovery and Attack, not reconstructed from memory afterward.

## 4. Why It's the "Compliance-Friendly" Standard

Because NIST SP 800-115 is published by a US federal standards body, it's frequently cited directly in regulatory and contractual language — an organization required to be "NIST-compliant" for a given framework can point to a NIST-aligned test as satisfying that requirement, which PTES/OSSTMM/OWASP (all industry-originated, not government) can't offer in the same way.

## 5. Interview Questions

1. What are the three categories of testing technique defined in NIST SP 800-115? → **Review Techniques (passive), Target Identification and Analysis Techniques (active discovery/scanning), and Target Vulnerability Validation Techniques (actual exploitation confirmation)**
2. Which category would a "vulnerability scan" fall under, and which would a full "penetration test" fall under? → **Vulnerability scan → Target Identification and Analysis; penetration test → Target Vulnerability Validation**
3. What are the four phases of the NIST SP 800-115 process? → **Planning, Discovery, Attack, Reporting**
4. Why is NIST SP 800-115 often specifically required in government/regulated environments over PTES or OSSTMM? → **It's published by a US federal standards body, making it directly citable for regulatory/contractual compliance requirements in a way industry-originated standards aren't**

## 6. Key Points

- NIST SP 800-115 organizes testing into **three technique categories**: review (passive), target identification/analysis (active, non-exploitative), and vulnerability validation (actual exploitation).
- Its four-phase process — **Planning, Discovery, Attack, Reporting** — treats reporting as continuous, not a final step.
- It's the standard most likely to be **explicitly required** in government, defense, and regulated-industry contracts.
- Complements rather than replaces PTES/OSSTMM/OWASP — organizations often blend NIST's compliance-friendly language with another standard's practical execution detail.
